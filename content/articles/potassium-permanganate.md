**Potassium permanganate**, **KMnO₄** (potassium manganate(VII)), is a strong
oxidising agent. Its deep purple colour comes from the $\ce{MnO4-}$ ion, which
makes it very easy to see when it reacts.

## In acidic solution

In dilute sulfuric acid, manganate(VII) is reduced all the way to $\ce{Mn^2+}$,
which is almost colourless:

$$
\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5e- -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O} \qquad E^\circ = +1.51\ \text{V}
$$

So the colour change is **purple → colourless**.

### Titrations

KMnO₄ is used in redox titrations, for example to find the amount of iron(II):

$$
\ce{MnO4- + 5Fe^2+ + 8H+ -> Mn^2+ + 5Fe^3+ + 4H2O}
$$

It's **self-indicating**: the end point is the first permanent pale pink colour,
because that's when there's no more $\ce{Fe^2+}$ left to use up the purple
permanganate. Sulfuric acid is used, not hydrochloric acid, because
$\ce{MnO4-}$ would oxidise $\ce{Cl-}$ to chlorine.

## In organic chemistry

**Cold, dilute, alkaline KMnO₄** adds two OH groups across a C=C bond to make a diol.
The purple colour turns into a brown precipitate of $\ce{MnO2}$:

$$
\ce{CH2=CH2 + [O] + H2O -> HOCH2CH2OH}
$$

This works as a test for alkenes.

**Hot, concentrated, acidified KMnO₄** is much harsher. It breaks the C=C bond
completely, giving ketones, carboxylic acids or $\ce{CO2}$ depending on what was
attached to each carbon.

It also oxidises any alkyl side chain on a benzene ring down to a $\ce{-COOH}$ group,
so methylbenzene becomes benzoic acid.

## Safety

KMnO₄ is a strong oxidiser and can start fires if mixed with organic materials. It
stains skin and clothes brown.
