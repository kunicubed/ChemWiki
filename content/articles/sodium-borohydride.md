**Sodium borohydride**, **NaBH₄**, is a mild reducing agent. It reduces
**aldehydes and ketones** to alcohols but leaves most other groups alone. Because it
is safe in water, it's the reagent used for carbonyl reductions at A level.

## What it does

Like [[Lithium aluminium hydride|LiAlH₄]], NaBH₄ gives a hydride ion, $\ce{H-}$,
which attacks the carbon of the C=O group. This is **nucleophilic addition**.

- Aldehyde → primary alcohol
- Ketone → secondary alcohol

$$
\ce{CH3CHO + 2[H] -> CH3CH2OH}
$$

$$
\ce{CH3COCH3 + 2[H] -> CH3CH(OH)CH3}
$$

## Mechanism

1. The hydride attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon, and the C=O π bond breaks so the
   oxygen gets a negative charge.
2. The negative oxygen picks up a proton from the water or alcohol solvent.

If the ketone is unsymmetrical, the hydride can attack from either face of the flat
C=O group, so you get a **racemic mixture** of the two enantiomers.

## NaBH₄ vs LiAlH₄

| | NaBH₄ | LiAlH₄ |
|---|---|---|
| Strength | Mild | Very strong |
| Aldehydes and ketones | Yes | Yes |
| Esters and acids | No | Yes |
| Solvent | Water or alcohol | Dry ether only |
| Reacts with water | Slowly | Violently |

Because it's selective, NaBH₄ is useful when a molecule has a ketone *and* an ester,
and you only want to reduce the ketone.

## Safety

NaBH₄ is much safer than LiAlH₄. It still releases hydrogen slowly in acid, and it's
harmful if swallowed.
