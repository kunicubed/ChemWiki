A **Grignard reagent** is an organomagnesium compound with the general formula
**RMgX**, where R is an alkyl or aryl group and X is a halogen. Grignard reagents are
one of the most useful ways to **make new carbon–carbon bonds**. Victor Grignard won
the 1912 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for discovering them.

## Making a Grignard reagent

A haloalkane is stirred with magnesium turnings in **dry ether** (or THF):

$$
\ce{CH3CH2Br + Mg ->[\text{dry ether}] CH3CH2MgBr}
$$

The ether has to be completely dry. Even a small amount of water destroys the reagent
(see below).

## Why it's a nucleophile

In a haloalkane the carbon is δ+. In a Grignard reagent the carbon is bonded to
magnesium, which is much less electronegative, so the carbon becomes **δ−**. It acts
a lot like a carbanion, $\ce{R-}$, and attacks electrophiles, especially the δ+ carbon
of a C=O group.

## Reactions with carbonyls

The Grignard adds to the C=O, then dilute acid is added to protonate the oxygen.
The type of alcohol you get depends on the carbonyl:

| React with | Product |
|---|---|
| Methanal (HCHO) | Primary alcohol |
| Other aldehyde | Secondary alcohol |
| Ketone | Tertiary alcohol |
| Ester | Tertiary alcohol (two R groups added) |
| Carbon dioxide | Carboxylic acid |

For example, with propanone:

$$
\ce{CH3MgBr + (CH3)2CO -> (CH3)3COMgBr ->[\ce{H3O+}] (CH3)3COH}
$$

With $\ce{CO2}$ (often as dry ice), the carbon chain gets one extra carbon:

$$
\ce{RMgBr + CO2 -> RCOOMgBr ->[\ce{H3O+}] RCOOH}
$$

## Why water ruins it

Grignard reagents are also very strong bases. Water (or any O–H or N–H group)
protonates them straight away and turns them into an alkane:

$$
\ce{CH3CH2MgBr + H2O -> CH3CH3 + Mg(OH)Br}
$$

So the starting materials can't contain alcohols, acids or amines, and the glassware
has to be dry.
