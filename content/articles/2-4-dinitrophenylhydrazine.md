**2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazine**, also called **2,4-DNPH** or **Brady's reagent**, is
used to test for a **C=O group in aldehydes and ketones**. A positive result is a
**yellow, orange or red precipitate**.

## The test

A few drops of the sample are added to Brady's reagent (2,4-DNPH dissolved in
methanol and sulfuric acid).

| Sample | Result |
|---|---|
| Aldehyde | Orange/yellow precipitate |
| Ketone | Orange/yellow precipitate |
| Carboxylic acid, ester, amide | No precipitate |

Carboxylic acids, esters and amides have a C=O too, but it's less reactive because
the lone pair on the neighbouring O or N delocalises into it.

## The reaction

The $\ce{-NH2}$ group of 2,4-DNPH adds to the C=O, then a molecule of water is lost.
This is called **condensation** (or *addition–elimination*):

$$
\ce{R2C=O + H2NNHC6H3(NO2)2 -> R2C=NNHC6H3(NO2)2 + H2O}
$$

The product is a **2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazone**, the coloured solid.

## Identifying the compound

2,4-DNPH only tells you there's an aldehyde or ketone, not which one. To identify it:

1. Filter off the precipitate.
2. Recrystallise it to make it pure.
3. Measure its **melting point**.
4. Compare with a data book. Each aldehyde and ketone gives a derivative with its own
   melting point.

To tell an aldehyde from a ketone, use [[Tollens' reagent]] or acidified
[[Potassium dichromate]].

## Safety

Dry 2,4-DNPH can explode on impact or friction, so it's stored and sold **wet**.
It's also toxic and an irritant.
