**Potassium dichromate(VI)**, **K₂Cr₂O₇**, is an orange oxidising agent. It is
usually used **acidified with dilute sulfuric acid**. Its main job in organic
chemistry is oxidising alcohols.

## The colour change

In acid, the orange dichromate ion is reduced to green chromium(III):

$$
\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H+ + 6e- -> 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O} \qquad E^\circ = +1.33\ \text{V}
$$

So a positive result is **orange → green**.

## Oxidising alcohols

What happens depends on the type of alcohol:

| Alcohol | Product | Colour change |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Aldehyde, then carboxylic acid | Orange → green |
| Secondary | Ketone | Orange → green |
| Tertiary | No reaction | Stays orange |

Tertiary alcohols don't react because the carbon with the OH has no hydrogen on it
to remove.

### Getting an aldehyde or an acid

For a primary alcohol, you choose the product by how you set up the reaction:

- **Distil** the mixture as it reacts. The aldehyde has a lower boiling point (no
  hydrogen bonding), so it boils off before it can be oxidised further.

$$
\ce{CH3CH2OH + [O] -> CH3CHO + H2O}
$$

- **Heat under reflux** with excess dichromate. The aldehyde can't escape, so it
  keeps reacting until it becomes the carboxylic acid.

$$
\ce{CH3CH2OH + 2[O] -> CH3COOH + H2O}
$$

A secondary alcohol only goes as far as the ketone:

$$
\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3 + [O] -> CH3COCH3 + H2O}
$$

## Other uses

Acidified dichromate can also tell aldehydes and ketones apart. Aldehydes turn it
green, ketones don't. Old breathalysers used the same colour change to detect
ethanol.

## Safety

Chromium(VI) compounds are **toxic and carcinogenic**. In research labs dichromate has
mostly been replaced by other oxidising agents such as [[Pyridinium chlorochromate|PCC]]
(which is also Cr(VI)) or chromium-free reagents.
