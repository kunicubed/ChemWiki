title: Organic Solvents
 

Solvents in organic chemistry are chosen and classified primarily on their polarity and hydrogen-bonding ability, which are independent and not mutually exclusive. The easiest way to determine whether a solvent is polar or non-polar is by looking at the locations of electronegative atoms (such as oxygen and nitrogen). If they are asymmetrically bonded, the solvent is polar. It is easier to determine whether a solvent is protic or aprotic: if it has O-H or N-H bonds, it is protic, and v.v.. 

Polar Aprotic Solvents: 

These solvents dissolve ionic / polar compounds well without the formation of hydrogen bonds, leaving nucleophiles exposed and highly reactive. These solvents are commonly used and found in SN2 reactions. 

Polar Protic Solvents: 

These solvents contain acidic protons (protons that can easily be donated to other species) capable of hydrogen bonding, and stabilising carbocations and anions. They are commonly found and used in SN1 and E1 pathways. 

 

## Tetrahydrofuran (THF):  


Tetrahydrofuran (THF) is a heterocyclic organic compound structured as a 4-carbon cyclic ether, with the formula C4H8O.  

The polarised C-O bonds gives THF a permanent dipole moment that allows it to dissolve a wide variety of both polar and non-polar compounds. THF cannot form hydrogen bonds with itself, as it lacks a hydrogen directly bonded to a highly electronegative atom. However, the 2 lone pairs on its oxygen can accept hydrogen bonds from other protic species like water, explaining its miscibility with H2O. 

Synthesis uses: 

In organic synthesis, THF is predominantly used in reactions involving organometallic reagents, namely Grignard reagents or organolithium compounds. The lone electron pairs on the oxygen atom allows THF to act as a Lewis base, stabilising electron-deficient metal cations through dative covalent bonding. This prevents the premature decomposition or precipitation of the reagent. 

Safety: 

THF is highly flammable with an extremely low flash point. Prolonged exposure to air and light can for heat and shock-sensitive explosive peroxides, especially when concentrated via distillation or evaporation. Never distil to dryness. Irritant, and is a GHS category 2 suspected carcinogen. Must be used in a fume cupboard. For fires, extinguish using dry chemicals, CO2, or alcohol-resistant foam. 

 

 

 

 

 

## Dimethylformamide (DMF) 

 

DMF consists of a formyl group (CHO) bonded to a nitrogen atom, which is further substituted with 2 methyl groups. It is an acyclic amide with the molecular formula C3H7NO. 

Due to resonance between the carbonyl oxygen and the nitrogen lone pair, the C-N bond possesses significant double-bond character. This restricts rotation around the C-N bond at room temperature, making the 2 methyl groups chemically non-equivalent (produces separate NMR signals) 

Synthesis uses: 

Effective at dissolving a wide array of polar organic compounds, ionic salts, and transition-metal catalysts due to its large dipole moment and high dielectric constant. DMF is ideal for SN2 reactions. Does not form H-bonds with nucleophiles as it is aprotic. Selectively solvates cations, leaving nucleophile exposed and highly reactive. High BP at 153C allows reactions using DMF to be conducted at elevated conditions at aqueous state. 

Safety: 

Skin-absorption hazards may cause liver damage and reproductive hazard. Irritant. Work under fume hood. 

 

## Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) 

 

Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) is an organosulfur compound with the molecular formula C2H6OS. It consists of a tetravalent S atom with sp3 hybridized orbitals at the centre, and a trigonal pyramidal shape. 

 

Synthesis uses: 

DMSO is a strongly polar aprotic solvent due to the polarised S=O bond. It has a high dielectric constant, translating to the ability to dissolve a broad spectrum of both polar and non-polar organic compounds, as well as inorganic salts. DMSO is frequently used for nucleophilic substitution reactions, particulary those involving alkali metal salts. DMSO’s strong cation-solvating ability leaves anions unhindered and ready to react. DMSO may also play a direct role as a chemical reagent, acting as a mild oxidant to convert primary and secondary alcohols into aldehydes and ketones under cryogenic conditions. 

Safety: 

Rapid absorption through skin, may cause toxic carrier effect. Irritant. Use in a well-ventilated area, preferably under a fume hood. 

 

## Acetonitrile (MeCN) 

 

Acetonitrile (MeCN) is a linear nitrile, consisting of a methyl group bonded to a cyano group. It has the molecular formula C2H3N. It is strongly polar and aprotic, due to the highly electronegative nitrogen atom. 

Synthesis uses: 

MeCN is chosen for SN2 reactions as it does not form H-bonds with nucleophiles, leaving them reactive and unhindered. It has a relatively low boiling point (82 C) and low viscosity, making it convenient for reactions that require moderate thermal conditions.  

Safety: 

Highly flammable. Toxic, may cause cyanide poisoning through inhalation, skin absorption, or digestion. Use under fume hood. 

 

## Acetone 

 

Acetone is the simplest and most common ketone, with the molecular formula C3H6O. It is a polar aprotic solvent due to the electronegative carbonyl oxygen. 

Synthesis uses: 

Acetone is very versatile. It can dissolve both polar and non-polar organic compounds, and is miscible with water in all proportions. Acetone is used for substitution reactions, oxidations, and as a polar rinsing agent for cleaning due to its high volatility. Its low boiling point allows for easy removal via rotary evaporation during workup. 

Safety: 

Heavy vapours are explosive. Irritant. Use under fume hood. 

 

## Methanol (MeOH), Ethanol (EtOH) 

 

Methanol and ethanol are the first 2 members of the alcohols homologous series, with the general formula CnH2n+1OH. They are both polar protic solvents due to their -OH group. They can both form hydrogen bonds with themselves, as well as with solute molecules. They are miscible with water. 

Synthesis uses: 

MeOH and EtOH both have relatively high dielectric constants, allowing them to solvate a large spectrum of polar and ionic species. MeOH is often used as a reaction medium for solvolysis, reductions involving NaBH4, and various transesterification processes. EtOH is used for nucleophilic substitutions, condensation reactions, and recrystallisation for purifying organic solids. It can also act as a weak acid to participate directly in reactions like acid-catalysed esterification. 

Safety: 

MeOH is acutely toxic. May cause blindness. EtOH is far less toxic, but both are flammable liquids. 


#Acetic acid (AcOH) 
 

Acetic acid (ethanoic acid, commonly abbreviated in organic chemistry as AcOH) is a polar protic solvent (albeit its dielectric constant is considerably lower than other polar solvents) and a weak carboxylic acid. In organic synthesis, it is frequently utilized as a reaction medium for acid-catalyzed transformations, such as Fischer esterifications, electrophilic aromatic substitutions (like nitrations and halogenations), and condensation reactions, where its acidic properties can simultaneously act as a proton source or catalyst. 

Because acetic acid possesses both a carbonyl group and a hydroxyl group, it forms strong intermolecular hydrogen-bonding networks (often existing as cyclic dimers in the liquid and vapor phases), resulting in a relatively high boiling point compared to alkanes of similar molecular mass.  

 

#Isopropanol (i-PrOH) 

 

Isopropanol (isopropyl alcohol, 2-propanol), often abbreviated as i-PrOH, is a secondary alcohol widely utilized as a polar protic solvent in organic synthesis and recrystallization procedures. Because of its secondary hydroxyl group, it forms hydrogen bonds and can solvate polar and ionic species effectively while remaining slightly less polar and less toxic than methanol. In synthetic applications, isopropanol is frequently used as a solvent for specific metal-catalyzed reductions—such as Meerwein-Ponndorf-Verley reductions—and as a washing or drying solvent during laboratory workups due to its favorable volatility and complete miscibility with both water and non-polar organic media.
